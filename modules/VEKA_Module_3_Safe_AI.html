🧠 Part 1: How AI Actually Works — What Happens to Your Data

▼

What is a Large Language Model (LLM)?

ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot and Gemini are Large Language Models. Think of them as extremely advanced autocomplete. They were trained on enormous quantities of text — books, websites, papers, forums — and learned the patterns in human language.

Key insight: LLMs don't "know" things the way a person does. They predict the most likely next word based on patterns they have seen. This is exactly why they can sound completely confident while being completely wrong — and why a fabricated U-value reads just as professionally as a real one.

Nothing in that chain checks whether the answer is true. There is no fact-checking step. Fluency and accuracy are separate things, and the model only optimises for one of them.

Why Does This Matter for a Profile Systems Business?

VEKA sits in an unusual position: a manufacturer with trade-secret process knowledge, selling through a competitive channel, into projects won on price. That combination produces data that is unusually valuable to the wrong reader.

🪟 Data That Is Uniquely Sensitive Here

  • Compound and formulation detail — the recipe is decades of R&D and is not patented in most of its detail
  • Extrusion parameters, die and tooling design — process know-how competitors cannot buy
  • Dealer and fabricator price lists, discount slabs, rebate schemes — reveal your entire commercial structure
  • Project quotations — you are bidding against other systems suppliers and against aluminium
  • Unreleased system designs and launch plans — competitive timing advantage
  • Fabricator performance and volume data — a competitor's poaching list

💥 Consequences Specific to This Business

  • Leaked discount structure → every dealer negotiation resets from your floor, permanently
  • Leaked project quotation → undercut on a multi-tower order
  • Invented performance figure in a proposal → a commitment you cannot meet, on a facade you cannot replace cheaply
  • Fabricated standard reference in a specification → rejected at approval stage, or worse, accepted and discovered later
  • Formulation or process detail exposed → an advantage you cannot get back
  • Employee personal data leaked → DPDP Act exposure, penalties up to ₹250 crore
💡 The asymmetry to hold on to: a leak takes two seconds and cannot be undone. Anonymising the same data takes two minutes. There is no version of this where the shortcut was worth it.

⚠️ Part 2: The 5 Categories of AI Risk at VEKA

▼

Risk 1: Data Leakage — Your Information Leaves Your Control

When you paste data into a consumer AI tool, it goes to a third party's servers, may be retained, and on some plans may be used to improve the model. Your dealer discount structure, a project quotation, or an unreleased system spec is then outside your control and outside your contracts.

Real-world parallel:

In 2023 Samsung restricted internal use of generative AI tools after engineers pasted internal code into ChatGPT. If that can happen at a company of that size with that much security investment, it can happen in a sales office. VEKA's equivalent is a project quotation or a discount matrix pasted in for a "quick summary".

Risk 2: Hallucination — AI Generates False Information

AI does not distinguish fact from fiction. It generates the most statistically likely text, not the most accurate. In fenestration, a fabricated thermal or acoustic figure, an invented standard clause, or a made-up warranty term becomes a written commercial commitment the moment it goes into a proposal.

Why it is worse in this domain than in general use:

Models hallucinate more where training data is thin. Indian fenestration standards, system-specific test data and fabricator-dependent performance are all thin. The general concept of a U-value is well represented in training data; your system's tested U-value with that glazing in that configuration is not. The model will produce a number anyway.

Risk 3: Over-Reliance — Trusting AI Without Verification

As teams get comfortable, they stop checking. This is the most dangerous risk because it is invisible until something goes wrong. A quotation total that is wrong, a technical claim nobody verified, a rejection email with a line that should never have been written — all because "the AI wrote it".

The human factor:

Structured, confident, well-formatted output triggers less scrutiny than a colleague's rough draft would. The better the formatting, the less it gets read. Watch for the moment your own review becomes a skim — that is the moment the risk arrives.

Risk 4: Compliance Violation — Breaking Rules Without Knowing

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 sets rules on how personal data may be processed. Uploading employee identity documents, salary data or candidate CVs to an external AI service can constitute unauthorised processing and transfer, with penalties running to very large sums.

Practical DPDP points for VEKA:

  • Consent and purpose: data collected for HR purposes cannot simply be reused for AI analysis
  • Third parties: an AI provider is a processor; your obligations do not disappear because a tool is convenient
  • Candidate data: a CV is someone else's personal data and you do not have consent to send it to a third party
  • Breach handling: if personal data leaks via an AI tool, there are notification obligations with tight timelines
  • Contractual overlay: separately from DPDP, your customer and supplier agreements may restrict sharing their information with third parties at all
Not legal advice. Have VEKA's legal team confirm how DPDP and your specific contracts apply before you set policy on the basis of a training module.

Risk 5: Ethical Misuse — Using AI Inappropriately

AI can be used to fabricate documents that look authentic. Even where technically possible, these uses are dishonest and frequently illegal.

Examples in this business:

  • Generating a test report or certificate that was never issued
  • Producing a performance claim for a marketing sheet with no test behind it
  • Drafting a sustainability or recycled-content claim that nothing supports
  • Creating a fabricated meeting record with invented attendees or decisions
  • Writing a technical justification designed to get a specification approved that should not be

Position to state plainly in the room: using AI to generate false, misleading or fraudulent documentation is a disciplinary matter and potentially a legal one. There is no ambiguity here and no grey area to explore.

💡 Key Teaching Point: these five risks are not theoretical. They occur routinely at organisations that adopt AI without governance. The goal of Module 3 is to keep the productivity gains from Sessions 1 and 2 while removing the tail risk that comes attached to them.

"The AI told me" is never an acceptable defence. The person who uses the output owns the outcome.

The Two Tags That Make Verification Possible

You cannot verify what you cannot see. Instruct the model to expose its own gaps, every time:

  • [confirm] — a fact the model does not have and you must supply
  • [assumption] — an inference it made, flagged for your approval
ADD TO EVERY HIGH-RISK PROMPT
Do not invent any figure, date, specification, standard reference, certification, warranty term or lead time. Where a value would strengthen this output but is not in what I gave you, write [confirm: what is needed and who supplies it] instead of a value. Mark any inference as [assumption: ...]. Cite the source line for every number you do use. At the end, list every [confirm] and [assumption] separately so I can work through them.
💡 Use tag count as a smoke alarm. If a real technical or commercial task comes back with zero [confirm] tags, that is not a clean bill of health — it almost certainly means something got filled in silently. Go and find it.

🌍 Part 4: Real-World AI Safety Incidents — Lessons from Industry

▼

Documented incidents at large organisations. They show what happens when AI is used without governance. Click each to expand.

GLOBAL Samsung — Internal Code Pasted Into ChatGPT

▼

What Happened

In 2023, Samsung semiconductor staff pasted internal source code and meeting content into ChatGPT to debug and summarise. The material left the company's control and sat on a third party's servers.

Impact

Samsung restricted internal use of generative AI tools and moved towards building internal alternatives. The precise competitive damage was never publicly quantified — which is itself part of the lesson. With this kind of exposure you rarely find out what it cost you, because you never see the competitor who benefited.

Lesson for VEKA

The people who did this were engineers at a company with serious security training, doing something that felt helpful. Nobody was being reckless. VEKA's equivalent is pasting a project quotation, a discount matrix or an extrusion parameter sheet in for a quick check. The intent is fine; the exposure is permanent.

GLOBAL Air Canada — Chatbot Gave a Wrong Policy, Company Paid

▼

What Happened

Air Canada's website chatbot told a customer he could book at full fare and apply for a bereavement discount afterwards. The airline's actual policy required applying before travel. The customer relied on the chatbot and was then refused.

Impact

Air Canada argued it should not be held responsible for what its chatbot said. In early 2024 a Canadian tribunal rejected that argument and ordered the airline to compensate the customer. The principle established was blunt: a company is responsible for the information it publishes, including AI-generated information.

Lesson for VEKA

If AI drafts a technical answer, a warranty statement or a delivery commitment that goes to a dealer, an architect or a homeowner — and it is wrong — VEKA owns it. "The tool generated it" is not a defence. This is the single strongest argument for the human sign-off gate, and it is worth reading aloud in the room.

GLOBAL New York Lawyer — Court Filing With Fabricated Cases

▼

What Happened

In 2023 a lawyer used ChatGPT to research precedents and filed a brief citing six cases. The citations had correct formatting, plausible names and realistic reasoning. He did not verify them.

Impact

The cases did not exist. The court sanctioned the lawyer and the story went worldwide. What made it possible was not carelessness about quality — the output looked better than genuine research, which is precisely why it went unchecked.

Lesson for VEKA

When AI produces a standard reference with a clause number, a test result, or a named certification, treat the specificity as a warning rather than as reassurance. AI fabricates technical references with exactly the same confidence it states real ones. A specification citing a clause nobody can find is the fenestration version of this case.

ILLUSTRATIVE Supplier Contract Breached by a Convenient Upload

▼

📍 Scenario

Staff at a services firm upload client deliverables to a consumer AI tool for review. The client's contract contains a confidentiality clause restricting disclosure to third parties, and a requirement about where data is processed. The AI provider's servers sit outside that boundary.

💥 Risk

The breach is contractual, not just technical. It exists the moment the upload happens, whether or not anything is ever "leaked" in the ordinary sense. Consequences typically include contract termination rights, an audit at your cost, and a relationship that never fully recovers.

Lesson for VEKA

This one is written as an illustration rather than a named incident, because the pattern matters more than the company. VEKA's version: a builder's tender documents, a customer's drawing set, or a supplier's technical data sheet uploaded for a quick summary. Before uploading anything that came from outside VEKA, check whether an agreement covers it. Usually one does.

🔒 Part 5: Data Classification — The 3-Tier System for VEKA

▼

🟢 Tier 1: Public / Safe

Share freely with any AI tool

  • General industry and technical knowledge
  • Anything already on the public VEKA website
  • Published brochures and customer-facing literature
  • Published standards and public regulations
  • Generic templates and checklists
  • Training material like this module

🟡 Tier 2: Internal — Anonymise

Share ONLY after removing identifiers

  • Sales data (remove dealer and project names)
  • Production output (remove line and plant identity)
  • Complaint records (remove customer and individual names)
  • Site photographs (remove logos, faces, signage)
  • Internal reports (remove names and exact figures)
  • Meeting notes (remove attendees and commercial detail)

🔴 Tier 3: Confidential — NEVER

NEVER upload to any external AI

  • Compound formulation and additive recipes
  • Extrusion parameters, die and tooling design
  • Dealer price lists, discount slabs, rebate schemes
  • Project quotations and pricing strategy
  • Employee salary, Aadhaar, PAN; candidate CVs
  • Customer and supplier contracts; unreleased system data

VEKA Anonymisation Guide

Original DataAnonymised VersionWhy
"Meridian Fenestration, Pune ordered 42 units""Dealer A, West region, ~40 units"Dealer identity and exact volume protected
"Skyline Towers project, 3 towers, Mumbai""Residential high-rise project, metro city"Customer and project identity protected
"Mr. Sharma, Regional Head, approved this""Regional management approved this"Individual identity protected
"West region did ₹4.2 Cr in July""Region 1 did index 100 in month 1"Shape of the data kept, commercial figure removed
"Our quotation: ₹1.85 Cr for the 3-tower order"⛔ NEVER SHARE — Tier 3A competitor needs only to be ₹1 lower
"Dealer slab: 18% base, 4% volume rebate above X"⛔ NEVER SHARE — Tier 3Exposes your entire commercial floor
"Compound: [additive package and ratios]"⛔ NEVER SHARE — Tier 3Trade secret. No business reason to ever paste this.
"Candidate CV: name, phone, salary history"⛔ NEVER SHARE — Tier 3Third-party personal data; no consent for this use

How to Anonymise in Practice — 4 Moves

  • Replace, don't delete. Dealer A/B/C, Project Alpha/Beta, Region 1/2/3. AI needs to know two things are different, not what they are called.
  • Index or scale the numbers. Divide everything by a factor only you know, or convert to an index where the largest is 100. Ratios and trends survive; the commercial figure does not.
  • Keep the structure, drop the identity. Column headings, units and relationships all matter to the analysis. Names and absolute values usually do not.
  • Do it in the file, not in your head. Find-and-replace on a copy of the spreadsheet takes two minutes and is repeatable. Retyping "carefully" is how one row slips through.
⚠️ Check for re-identification. "Dealer A, 42 units, Pune, uPVC, last week" identifies exactly one dealer to anyone in the industry. Anonymising the name is not enough if the combination of remaining details points to one company. Remove the location or the timing too.
💡 The VEKA Rule: if you would not print it and leave it on the table at a competitor's office, do not paste it into a consumer AI tool.

📂 Part 6: VEKA Case Studies — Data Leak Scenarios

▼

Fictional but realistic scenarios modelled on VEKA's actual business. All names and figures are synthetic. Click each to expand.

DATA LEAK CS-1: The Discount Matrix Pasted In for a "Quick Table"

▼

📍 Scenario

A channel sales manager wants a cleaner version of the dealer discount structure for a regional review. He pastes the whole matrix into a free AI tool — base discount by slab, volume rebate thresholds, special terms for three named key accounts — and asks it to format the table nicely.

💥 Risk

This is the most commercially damaging single paste available to anyone in the sales organisation. It is your entire pricing floor, your incentive design, and which accounts get special treatment — in one block. A competitor with this document knows exactly what to offer every dealer you have. And unlike a lost order, you would never know it happened.

✅ Fix

Formatting a table needs no real numbers at all. Replace every figure with a placeholder, get the structure back, then fill the real values in yourself in Excel. Or just do it in Excel. Time cost of doing it safely: about the same. Risk: zero.

DATA LEAK CS-2: The Builder's Tender Documents Uploaded for a Summary

▼

📍 Scenario

A project sales executive receives a 60-page tender pack from a developer — drawings, specification, commercial conditions, penalty clauses. Rather than read it, he uploads the whole PDF and asks: "What are the fenestration requirements and what are the penalties for delay?"

💥 Risk

Two separate problems. First, the developer's confidential tender documents are now on a third party's servers, and the tender terms almost certainly prohibit that — a breach that exists whether or not anything is ever exposed. Second, VEKA's interest in this project and the commercial terms it is working with have left the building. Consequences range from disqualification from the tender to a damaged relationship with a developer you want repeat business from.

✅ Fix

Search the PDF for the clause you need — that is thirty seconds. If you genuinely need AI help interpreting a clause, extract that single paragraph, remove party names and project identity, and paste only that. On enterprise tooling with the right agreement, more is possible — but check the tender terms first, not after. Documents that arrived from outside VEKA are the highest-risk uploads in the company, because someone else's contract governs them.

DATA LEAK CS-3: Candidate CVs Screened in a Consumer Tool

▼

📍 Scenario

HR has 40 CVs for a technical sales role and a deadline. Someone uploads all 40 — names, phone numbers, addresses, current employers, salary history — and asks the AI to rank them against the job description.

💥 Risk

Forty people's personal data, belonging to them and not to VEKA, sent to a third party with no consent for that purpose. This sits squarely in DPDP territory, with substantial penalty exposure. There is a second problem alongside it: an unexamined AI ranking can encode bias against gaps in employment, non-metro colleges, or unfamiliar company names — and produce a hiring decision nobody can explain if challenged.

✅ Fix

Never upload a CV. Instead, build the scorecard first (Module 3 lab), then extract only anonymised, job-relevant attributes yourself: "Candidate 1: 6 years technical sales, fenestration background, handled 20+ dealer accounts, comfortable on site." Rank those. The AI adds the same structure and you have disclosed nothing. The final shortlist decision stays with a human either way.

DATA LEAK CS-4: The Formulation Question

▼

📍 Scenario

A plant engineer is troubleshooting a surface finish issue. He pastes the compound formulation — additive package, ratios, supplier grades — plus current extrusion line parameters, and asks the AI what might be causing it.

💥 Risk

This is the nuclear scenario for a manufacturer. Formulation and process knowledge is the accumulated result of decades of development. Much of it is protected as trade secret rather than by patent, which means its protection depends entirely on it never leaving the company. Once pasted, that protection is gone and cannot be restored. Unlike a lost order, there is no recovery and no version of this you can fix later.

✅ Fix

Ask the question generically: "What process factors typically cause this class of surface defect in PVC profile extrusion?" You get the same diagnostic thinking with none of your specifics. Then apply it internally against your own data. Formulation and process parameters are Tier 3 permanently, on every tool, including enterprise ones, unless R&D and legal have specifically approved otherwise.

BEST PRACTICE CS-5: Regional Sales Analysis Done Right

▼

📍 Scenario

An MIS analyst needs to understand why one region is underperforming. She spends eight minutes on a copy of the file: dealer names become Dealer A–P, regions become Region 1–4, and all values are converted to an index where the highest month is 100. Column headings, units and the time series stay exactly as they were. Then she uploads it.

📊 Result

The analysis found that the underperformance sat in a small cluster of dealers rather than across the region, and flagged a pattern in order timing nobody had looked at. Analysis quality was identical to using real names — because the shape of the data carries the insight, and the identities never did. Eight minutes of preparation; nothing disclosed. This is the pattern to copy.

✅ Why it worked

She anonymised in the file, not in her head. She kept structure and dropped identity. And she checked the result for re-identification before uploading — Region 1 with sixteen dealers is not identifiable; a region with two would have been.

🔍 Part 7: AI Hallucinations — Fenestration-Specific Dangers

▼
⚠️ AI can state things confidently that are entirely wrong. In this business, an invented number does not stay a mistake — it becomes a written commitment on a facade that costs a great deal to replace.

HALLUCINATION CS-6: The Invented Standard Clause

▼

📍 Scenario

A technical executive asks an AI tool what the applicable standard requires for uPVC window water tightness. The answer arrives with a standard number, a specific clause reference in the form "Clause 7.4.2(b)", a test pressure and a pass criterion. It reads exactly like a technical extract. He pastes it into a specification response to an architect.

💥 Problem

The standard exists. The clause number does not. The test pressure came from a different context entirely. The architect's consultant tried to look up the clause during specification review, could not find it, and queried it — which meant VEKA's entire technical submission was then read with suspicion. The cost is not just the rework; it is that a specifier who has caught you once reads everything you send differently afterwards.

✅ How to Catch It

Never let a clause number leave the building unverified. Look it up in the actual standard document, every time. Better prompt: "Explain what water tightness testing assesses and why it matters. Do not cite clause numbers or test values — I will take those from the standard and our data sheets." Ask AI for the concept; take the numbers from the source.

HALLUCINATION CS-7: A U-Value That Nobody Tested

▼

📍 Scenario

A sales engineer is building a proposal for a residential project and needs a thermal performance figure. He asks the AI what U-value the system achieves. It returns a specific number to one decimal place, plus a comparison stating the system performs a given percentage better than aluminium.

💥 Problem

Both numbers are invented. Worse, the question was unanswerable in principle: the thermal performance of an installed window depends on the profile system, the glazing unit, the spacer, the reinforcement, the size and configuration, and the installation detail. No single number exists for "the system" in isolation. By putting a figure in a proposal, VEKA made a performance commitment on a project of hundreds of units. If the built windows are later tested and fall short, that is a warranty conversation on a facade.

✅ How to Catch It

Any performance figure comes from a test report for a defined configuration, or it does not go in the document. Instruct the agent: "Never state a thermal, acoustic, wind load or water tightness value. Write [confirm: which test report and configuration] instead." Then the proposal arrives with a gap you have to fill — which is exactly what you want it to do. The most dangerous questions are the ones with no valid single answer, because AI will always produce one anyway.

HALLUCINATION CS-8: The Warranty Term That Was Never Offered

▼

📍 Scenario

AI drafts a dealer proposal email. Nobody mentioned warranty in the prompt. The draft includes a sentence offering a ten-year warranty against warping and discolouration. It reads well, so it stays. The email goes out.

💥 Problem

VEKA has now made a written warranty commitment in terms nobody in the company approved, to a customer who will reasonably rely on it. The actual warranty position depends on the system, the market, the fabricator's own workmanship warranty and the installation. Softening it later to "up to ten years" does not undo a written statement already sent. This is the most common hallucination in commercial writing and the easiest to miss, precisely because it makes the email sound stronger.

✅ How to Catch It

Read every AI-drafted customer document looking specifically for commitments, not errors. Warranty, delivery, price, performance. Anything in one of those four categories that you did not put in the prompt was invented. Run the adversarial pass: "List every sentence that states or implies a commercial or technical commitment, and what evidence would be needed to keep it."

HALLUCINATION CS-9: Quotation Total Wrong by ₹6.4 Lakh

▼

📍 Scenario

An estimator pastes a 38-line schedule of window types, quantities and rates into an AI tool and asks it to total the order value. It returns a clean figure. That figure goes into the quotation.

💥 Problem

Manual checking in Excel found the AI had mis-multiplied rate by quantity on three lines. The gap was ₹6.4 lakh (synthetic figure) on a mid-sized order — enough to turn a thin-margin job into a loss-making one, and enough that discovering it after the customer accepts leaves you with no good options. Language models are pattern engines, not calculators; arithmetic across many rows is a known weak point.

✅ How to Catch It

Do final arithmetic in Excel. Always. Use AI to structure the schedule, spot a missing window type, or sanity-check that a rate looks out of line — never for the number that goes in the quotation. A two-minute cross-check catches errors worth lakhs, and it never gets cheaper than two minutes.

HALLUCINATION CS-10: AI "Approves" a High-Rise Configuration

▼

📍 Scenario

An application engineer describes a proposed window configuration in text — size, opening type, floor level, coastal location — and asks whether it is suitable for the wind loads on an 18-floor coastal tower. The AI responds that the configuration appears suitable, with a short explanation.

💥 Problem

The AI processed a sentence, not a structural condition. Wind load suitability depends on design wind pressure at that height and exposure, the reinforcement specification, the profile section properties, the glazing, the anchoring, and the fabricator's execution. None of that was available to it. What it produced was a confident-sounding sentence that gives false comfort to the one person in the chain who most needed to escalate. If the configuration is built and fails in service on a coastal high-rise, the consequences are not commercial.

✅ How to Catch It

Structural suitability is Trust Level 4 — AI output is not an input to it. Only a qualified engineer working from actual design wind pressures and the reinforcement specification can make this call. Instruct any agent: "If a question requires a structural, wind load or safety determination, do not answer. State what information a qualified engineer would need and stop." An AI that says "escalate this" is working correctly.

HALLUCINATION CS-11: A Fabricator's Track Record, Entirely Invented

▼

📍 Scenario

Before appointing a new fabricator, a channel manager asks the AI what it knows about "Crestline Windows Pvt Ltd" — projects completed, reliability, certifications. It returns a detailed profile: eight years in business, four named projects, a quality certification, a good reputation in the region.

💥 Problem

Crestline Windows is a name invented for this workshop. Every detail in that profile was fabricated. If a manager relied on it to appoint a fabricator, VEKA would be putting its brand on windows made by a company with an unknown track record — and brand damage from bad fabrication is the hardest kind to repair, because the homeowner blames the profile.

✅ How to Catch It

Never use AI to verify that an organisation exists or is any good. Instead: ask for documents directly, call references yourself, check company registry filings, visit the workshop, look at their machines and their finished work. AI cannot verify real-world entities. It generates plausible profiles, and a plausible profile is worse than no information, because it stops you looking.

HALLUCINATION CS-12: A Sustainability Claim With Nothing Behind It

▼

📍 Scenario

Marketing asks AI to draft copy for a sustainability page. The draft includes a recycled content percentage, a lifecycle claim, and a reference to an environmental certification scheme. It is well written and nobody questions the specifics.

💥 Problem

Environmental claims are among the most heavily scrutinised statements a manufacturer can publish, and an unsupported one is a regulatory and reputational risk in most markets, not merely an inaccuracy. A recycled content figure has to trace to actual material flows. A certification reference has to be a certification you actually hold. AI produced all three because they made the page better.

✅ How to Catch It

Treat every environmental claim as Trust Level 4. AI can draft the structure and the tone; every factual claim comes from a document with a name and a date, verified by whoever owns sustainability reporting. Instruct: "Do not state any recycled content figure, lifecycle claim or certification. Write [confirm: which report and who owns it]."

Comparative percentages"40% better than aluminium"Ask: better than which section, tested how?
Confident "always" or "never""The standard always requires..."Standards have conditions and exceptions
Warranty or lead time you didn't mention"10-year warranty", "dispatch in 4 weeks"Invented. Delete, then check who can approve it.
Detailed company or dealer profiles"They completed four projects and hold..."Verify independently — AI fabricates these
Round-number estimates"Exactly ₹5,00,000 per floor"Real commercial figures are rarely round
Any answer to an unanswerable question"Yes, this configuration is suitable"If it couldn't know, it didn't know
💡 The pattern across all eight rows: the hallucinated version always reads more professional than the honest one. Confidence and specificity are what invention looks like. Learn to find them suspicious.

📜 Part 9: VEKA AI Usage Policy (Draft)

▼
This is a starting draft for discussion, not an approved policy. It needs review by IT, legal and HR, and sign-off by leadership, before it means anything. Use this session to mark it up.

Draft AI Usage Policy — VEKA

Section 1: Approved AI Tools

  • Approved enterprise tools — Tier 1 and Tier 2 data
  • Consumer or free tools — Tier 1 only, and anonymised Tier 2
  • Microsoft Copilot — internal documents within the VEKA tenant
  • All other AI tools, browser extensions and plug-ins require IT approval before use

Section 2: Data Handling

  • Tier 1 (Public): use freely
  • Tier 2 (Internal): must be anonymised before any consumer tool; may be used as-is on approved enterprise tools
  • Tier 3 (Confidential): never uploaded to any external AI, including enterprise tools, without specific written approval
  • Documents received from customers, developers or suppliers: check the governing agreement before uploading anything
  • Default: when unsure, treat it as Tier 2 and anonymise

Section 3: Verification Requirements

  • Every AI output used in an official document requires human verification
  • Technical claims and standards references → qualified engineer sign-off
  • Performance, wind load and structural questions → never AI-answered; escalate
  • Commercial figures → cross-checked in Excel or the source system
  • Customer-facing documents → reviewed by the named document owner before sending
  • Environmental and certification claims → verified by whoever owns sustainability reporting

Section 4: Prohibited Uses

  • Uploading compound formulations, extrusion parameters, die or tooling design
  • Uploading dealer price lists, discount slabs, rebate schemes or project quotations
  • Uploading employee personal data, or candidate CVs
  • Uploading customer, developer or supplier contracts and tender documents
  • Using AI output as an engineering, structural or safety determination
  • Generating certificates, test reports, performance claims or environmental claims that are not backed by a real document

Section 5: Accountability

  • The person using AI is fully responsible for verifying the output
  • "The AI generated it" is never an acceptable explanation
  • Report a suspected data exposure to IT immediately, and without fear of blame — late reporting is the bigger problem
  • Every published agent or shared prompt has a named owner
  • Quarterly review of AI usage by function heads
