🧠 Part 1: What AI Is Good At — and Where It Fails

▼

The One-Sentence Version

AI is very good at transforming text you give it. It is unreliable at supplying facts you didn't give it. Almost every good use and every disaster in this programme comes from that one distinction.

You supply the facts
AI structures, drafts, reformats
vs
AI supplies the facts
it invents, confidently
✅ Best Practice: When you provide the exact context (dealer history, specific data points), the AI acts strictly as an intelligent formatter and editor. This eliminates hallucinations.
❌ High Risk: If you ask the AI to "write a sales pitch for a generic uPVC window," it will invent facts, numbers, and features that may not apply to VEKA products at all.

Every prompt you write should push the work towards the green side. (Click nodes for details)

Where It Genuinely Helps at VEKA

✅ Strong use cases

  • Rewriting — a rough note into a professional email
  • Restructuring — messy meeting notes into minutes with owners
  • Extracting — pulling requirements out of a long specification
  • Explaining — a technical concept translated for a homeowner or a new joiner
  • Comparing — two options laid out side by side on stated criteria
  • Drafting the boring 80% — the structure of a report you then fill in
  • Critiquing your own draft — finding what you missed before your manager does

❌ Where it fails, predictably

  • Any performance figure — thermal, acoustic, wind load, water tightness
  • Standard and clause references — it will invent clause numbers that look real
  • Arithmetic across many rows — quotation totals go wrong quietly
  • Facts about real companies — dealer track records get fabricated wholesale
  • Lead times, prices, warranty terms — invented to make a draft sound better
  • Anything requiring judgement it can't have — is this configuration suitable, is this person right for the role
⚠️ Note the pattern in the right-hand column. These are not random errors. They are the specific things a fluent writer would add to make a document more persuasive. AI adds them for the same reason — and that is why the failures cluster in exactly the places where being wrong is expensive.

The Mental Model to Carry Into Every Prompt

Treat AI as a fast, capable, extremely well-read new joiner on their first day — one who has never seen a VEKA document, has no access to your systems, will never admit they don't know something, and writes beautifully.

That framing tells you what to do. You brief them properly. You give them the source material. You tell them what good looks like. And you read what they produce before it goes anywhere, because their name isn't on it — yours is.

🔧 Part 2: The 5 Parts of a Working Prompt

▼

Anatomy — and What Happens When You Skip One

PartWhat it doesWhat happens if you leave it out
1. ROLETells it whose expertise and vocabulary to useGeneric tone; explains things your audience already knows
2. CONTEXTThe actual facts — the source material it works fromIt invents context. This is where most damage happens.
3. TASKThe one thing you want done, as a verbYou get a description of the problem instead of a solution
4. CONSTRAINTSLength, tone, audience, and what it must not doSix paragraphs where you needed four lines; invented figures
5. FORMATThe shape of the output — email, table, checklist, minutesProse you then have to reformat by hand
💡 If you only remember one: remember CONTEXT. A prompt with weak role and no format still produces something workable. A prompt with no context produces confident fiction.

Built Live — the Same Task, Five Times

Trainer builds this on screen, adding one part at a time, running it after each addition. The room watches the output improve.

1

Task only

"Write a follow-up email about a quote."

Result: a generic template with square brackets everywhere. Useless, but harmless.

2

Add ROLE

"You are a technical sales manager at a uPVC window systems company. Write a follow-up email about a quote."

Result: better vocabulary, right register. Still no substance — and now it starts inventing substance.

3

Add CONTEXT — the big jump

"...Quote sent 21 days ago to Dealer A: 42 window units, 70mm system. Last call: their site handover slipped by a month. Their client is comparing against a local aluminium fabricator."

Result: suddenly specific and usable. This single addition does more than the other four combined.

4

Add CONSTRAINTS

"...Under 140 words. Open by referencing their timeline slip, not 'just checking in'. Do not state any price, delivery date, warranty term or performance figure — write [confirm: ...] instead. One question at the end that can be answered in a line."

Result: tight, honest, and the gaps are now visible instead of filled in silently.

5

Add FORMAT

"Output: subject line, email body, then a bullet list of every [confirm] you used."

Result: ready to paste, plus a to-do list of what you must verify. This is the finished shape.

THE FINISHED PROMPT — COPY-PASTE
ROLE: You are a technical sales manager at a uPVC window systems company that supplies profile systems to fabricators and dealers. CONTEXT (synthetic — treat as the only source of truth): - Dealer A, West region - Quote sent 21 days ago: 42 window units, 70mm system - Last call: their site handover slipped by about a month - Their client is also evaluating a local aluminium fabricator TASK: Draft a follow-up email. CONSTRAINTS: - Maximum 140 words - Open by referencing their timeline slip, not "just checking in" - Do NOT state any price, discount, delivery date, lead time, warranty term or performance figure. Where one would help, write [confirm: what is needed and who supplies it] instead. - Mark any inference about their situation as [assumption: ...] - Give one concrete reason to reply this week that is not a discount - End with a single question answerable in one line FORMAT: subject line, then email body, then a bullet list of every [confirm] and [assumption] you used and why.
✅ The teaching point: nobody needs to memorise five labels. What they need to notice is that the output got dramatically better at step 3, and got safe at step 4. Context makes it useful; constraints make it trustworthy.

The Pocket Template

For anyone who wants one line to remember:

You are [role]. Here is what I have: [paste your facts]. [Verb] a [output type] for [audience]. Keep it [length and tone]. Do not invent figures — write [confirm: ...] for anything you don't have. Format as [shape].

👥 Part 3: Bad vs Good — Six VEKA Role Scenarios

PROJECT SALES Turning a site meeting into a follow-up the customer replies to

▼
PROJECT SALES EXECUTIVE

Context: You've come back from a meeting with a developer's project team. You have half a page of notes on your phone. You need a follow-up email out today that keeps the conversation moving.

❌ VAGUE PROMPT
Write a follow-up email after my site meeting today. Make it professional and persuasive.
✓ WORKING PROMPT
You are a project sales manager at a uPVC window systems company. My raw notes from today (synthetic): "Met project team, Tower B and C. Architect wants slim sightlines. PM worried about installation sequencing with facade contractor. Asked about acoustic performance - highway side. Handover target Q1. They've shortlisted two other suppliers. Next step: they send us the window schedule." Draft a follow-up email, max 150 words: - Confirm the three things they raised - Confirm the next step and who owns it - Do NOT state any acoustic, thermal or delivery figure. Write [confirm: ...] - One question that moves it forward Then list separately: everything I need to chase internally before I reply.
📝Paste the raw notes. Ugly, half-finished notes are perfect input. The messier your notes, the more value you get.
🎯Ask it to confirm what they raised. This is the move that makes a follow-up feel like you were listening.
🚫Block the acoustic figure explicitly. Otherwise it will supply one, because it makes the email stronger.
✅Ask for the internal chase list. The second output is often more useful than the email.

TECHNICAL Reading an architect's specification properly

▼
APPLICATION ENGINEER

Context: A specification has arrived. It's vague in the places that matter and precise in places that don't. You need to know what it actually commits you to and what you must ask before anyone quotes.

❌ VAGUE PROMPT
Is this specification okay for our system? [pastes spec]
✓ WORKING PROMPT
You are a senior application engineer for uPVC window systems. SPECIFICATION EXTRACT (synthetic): "uPVC windows with multi-chamber profile, thermal break, reinforced sections. 5-year warranty. Suitable for coastal exposure. Hardware to be European. Colour: white." Produce three things: 1. WHAT THIS ACTUALLY SPECIFIES - listed plainly, no interpretation 2. WHAT IT DOES NOT SPECIFY but must, before anyone can quote or commit - as a numbered query list I can send back to the architect 3. WHERE IT IS INTERNALLY VAGUE OR POTENTIALLY CONTRADICTORY RULES: - Never state a performance value, standard clause or warranty term. - Never say this "is compliant" with anything. - Anything requiring engineer sign-off: mark [escalate: ...]
🔍Ask what's missing, not whether it's okay. "Is this okay?" invites a yes. "What does this not specify?" produces the query list you actually needed.
⛔Ban the word "compliant". AI will use it, and it is not AI's call to make.
📤Output 2 is a deliverable. A numbered query list to the architect is the fastest way to look like the supplier who knows what they're doing.
🚩Add [escalate]. Trains the habit before Session 3 formalises it.

CHANNEL The objection you hear every single week

▼
DEALER / CHANNEL MANAGER

Context: "You're more expensive than the local fabricator." You've answered this a hundred times, differently each time. You want a response you can rely on that doesn't over-promise.

❌ VAGUE PROMPT
How do I answer when a dealer says we're too expensive?
✓ WORKING PROMPT
You are a channel sales trainer for a uPVC profile systems company in India. Build me an objection response with four parts: 1. REFRAME — one line that changes the frame. No numbers. 2. EVIDENCE NEEDED — the specific document or reference project I must have in hand before I say anything quantitative. Phrase each as [confirm: ...]. 3. NEVER CLAIM — statements someone might be tempted to make here that would be a performance, warranty or compliance commitment we cannot verify. 4. FOLLOW-UP QUESTION — one question that moves the conversation on. OBJECTION: "You're 20% more expensive than the local fabricator." Do not state any figure, percentage, standard or warranty term anywhere. If a figure feels necessary, it belongs in EVIDENCE NEEDED.
🛑The "NEVER CLAIM" column is the whole point. Nobody builds it, and it's the one that keeps you out of trouble. Read yours aloud to a colleague.
📄Force evidence into its own column. Stops the reframe becoming a claim.
♻️Build this once for your top five objections. It's a bank you'll use for a year, not a one-off answer.
🚫Never let it discuss a competitor's product. Keep it on what our own documents say about our own systems.

HR A job description you can actually interview against

▼
HUMAN RESOURCES

Context: Most JDs get copied from a competitor's careers page, which means you end up interviewing against someone else's idea of the role. Write the JD and the scorecard from the same brief and the questions actually test what you said you wanted.

❌ VAGUE PROMPT
Write a job description for a technical sales engineer.
✓ WORKING PROMPT
You are an experienced HR business partner in Indian manufacturing. BRIEF (synthetic): Role: Technical Sales Engineer, one region. Reports to Regional Sales Manager. Territory: 3 cities, roughly 25 active fabricator relationships. Hard part of the job: translating architect specifications into system recommendations, and coaching dealers' teams on installation quality. Non-negotiable: comfortable on a live construction site. PART A - JOB DESCRIPTION, max 300 words - Lead with the 3 outcomes this person owns in year one, not a duty list - Separate "must have" from "will teach". Max 4 must-haves. - No salary, band, or headcount - Anything not in the BRIEF: [confirm: ...]. Do not guess years of experience or qualifications. PART B - INTERVIEW SCORECARD One row per must-have. Columns: what we're testing, the question to ask, what a strong answer contains, what a weak answer contains. Behavioural or scenario-based questions only. PART C - BIAS AND EXCLUSION CHECK Review your own PART A. List wording that could unnecessarily narrow the pool: gendered language, age or marital proxies, unnecessary degree requirements, "culture fit" phrasing, physical requirements not genuinely essential. Suggest a replacement for each.
🎯Outcomes, not duties. "Owns dealer conversion in three cities" beats "responsible for sales activities".
🔗JD and scorecard from one brief. That's what makes the interview test the thing you advertised.
⚖️Part C is the one to read carefully. Ask yourself how many of your current JDs would survive it.
🔒Never paste a real CV, salary or candidate detail. Not today, not ever. Session 3 explains the legal side.

PLANT Shift notes into a handover anyone can act on

▼
PRODUCTION / PLANT

Context: Shift handover happens verbally and half of it gets lost. You want a written note in a consistent shape that the next shift and the plant head can both use.

❌ VAGUE PROMPT
Make this into a proper shift report: "line 2 down for die change, output was low, one first aid case"
✓ WORKING PROMPT
You are a production supervisor writing a shift handover note for a PVC profile extrusion plant. RAW NOTES (synthetic): "Shift B. Line 2 down 3 hrs for die change, started late because tooling wasn't pre-heated. Output 88% of plan. Line 1 and 3 normal. One minor first-aid case - operator cut hand on trim edge, treated, back on shift. Scrap up on line 2 first hour after restart. Raw material delivery expected tomorrow morning." FORMAT as a shift handover with these headings: Shift | Lines status | Output vs plan | Downtime and cause | Quality notes | Safety | Open items for next shift | Escalations RULES: - Use only what's in the notes. Anything missing: [missing - not stated]. - Do NOT suggest a root cause for the scrap or the injury. Record what was observed only. - Anything needing a decision above supervisor level: [escalate: ...] - Under one page.
🚫Block root-cause guessing. AI will confidently attribute the scrap to the die change. Recording observations and diagnosing are two different jobs.
📋Fix the headings. Same shape every shift is what makes the notes comparable over a month.
🩹Safety entries record facts, not conclusions. No names, no fault, no speculation.
🕳️[missing] is a feature. It tells the next shift what nobody wrote down.

MARKETING Explaining the product to someone who isn't technical

▼
MARKETING / CUSTOMER CARE

Context: A homeowner asks why uPVC and not aluminium. Your technical literature answers it for an engineer. You need the version a homeowner understands, without knocking the competition or promising anything.

❌ VAGUE PROMPT
Write marketing copy explaining why uPVC windows are better than aluminium.
✓ WORKING PROMPT
You are writing for a homeowner in India, no technical background, choosing windows for a flat. TASK: Explain in plain language what questions they should ask when choosing a window system, and what each one affects in daily life (comfort, noise, maintenance, monsoon). CONSTRAINTS: - Do NOT compare materials or brands, and do not criticise any alternative. - Do NOT state any performance figure, certification, warranty or lifespan. Write [confirm: ...] if one seems needed. - Do NOT make any environmental or recycled-content claim. - Max 250 words. Short sentences. No jargon - if a technical term is unavoidable, explain it in the same sentence. FORMAT: 5 questions, each with a one-line "why this matters to you".
🙋Teach, don't sell. "Questions to ask" outperforms "why we're better", and it's far safer.
🌱Block environmental claims explicitly. AI generates recycled-content percentages readily, and unsupported green claims are a regulatory risk, not just an error.
⚔️Never let it write comparative copy. Competitor comparisons in marketing material need legal review, always.
🗣️Name the reader. "A homeowner choosing windows for a flat" produces completely different copy than "a customer".

♻️ Part 4: The 6 Patterns That Cover Most of Your Work

▼

You do not need a new prompt for every task. Six shapes cover the large majority of writing and thinking work at VEKA. Learn the shapes; change the content.

Pattern 1 — REWRITE

You already have the content. You want it in a different voice, length or register. The safest pattern there is, because you supplied every fact.

PATTERN
Rewrite the text below for [audience], in [tone], under [length]. TEXT: [paste] Change wording and structure only. Do not add any fact, figure or claim that is not already in the text. If something is unclear, list it separately at the end rather than guessing.

Pattern 2 — EXTRACT

A long document, and you need the four things in it that matter to you. Turns 40 pages into a table.

PATTERN
From the document below, extract only the following: - [thing 1] - [thing 2] - [thing 3] DOCUMENT: [paste] Output as a table. One row per item found. Include a column giving the section or page it came from. If something on my list is not in the document, say so explicitly rather than inferring it. Do not summarise anything I did not ask for.

Pattern 3 — COMPARE

Two options, and you want them laid out on the same criteria instead of two paragraphs of prose.

PATTERN
Compare [option A] and [option B] on exactly these criteria: [criterion 1], [criterion 2], [criterion 3]. WHAT I KNOW: [paste everything you have] Output as a table, criteria as rows, options as columns. Where you do not have information for a cell, write [confirm: what is needed] — do not fill it with a general assumption. At the end, state which criteria the decision actually turns on.
💡 The last line is the useful one. Asking which criteria the decision turns on often reveals that four of your six criteria don't matter.

Pattern 4 — TRANSLATE FOR AN AUDIENCE

The same information, pitched at a fabricator's owner, a site engineer, an architect and a homeowner. Four different documents.

PATTERN
Explain the content below to [specific audience: e.g. "a homeowner with no technical background", "a site engineer who knows construction but not fenestration"]. CONTENT: [paste] Rules: - Assume they know [what they know] and do not know [what they don't] - Explain any unavoidable technical term in the same sentence - Do not add any figure or claim not in the CONTENT - [length] Then tell me which parts I should expect them to push back on.

Pattern 5 — BUILD A CHECKLIST

Turns a process that lives in someone's head into something repeatable that a colleague could run.

PATTERN
Build a checklist for [task], for someone doing it for the first time. WHAT I KNOW ABOUT THE TASK: [paste, however rough] Rules: - Every item must be checkable — someone can say done or not done - Order matters: sequence the items as they actually happen - Mark any item that needs someone else's approval [sign-off: who] - Maximum [n] items. If you need more, tell me what you cut. Then list what you were unsure about, so I can fill the gaps.

Pattern 6 — CRITIQUE MY DRAFT

The most underused pattern, and often the highest value. You write it; AI finds what you missed.

PATTERN
Review my draft below as a hostile reader whose job is to find where it would embarrass me. DRAFT: [paste] WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW: [paste your source facts] List every sentence that: (a) states or implies a technical performance claim (b) states or implies a commercial commitment — price, discount, delivery, warranty (c) asserts something I did not tell you For each: quote it, and say what evidence would be needed to keep it. Then tell me which single one is most likely to cause a real problem, and why.
✅ Run this on anything customer-facing. It catches the invented lead time, the accidental warranty, and the claim you didn't notice you'd made. Five seconds of work, and it is the single highest-return prompt on this page.

🏋️ Live Round — 15 Minutes, One Pattern Each

Facilitator calls out a pattern; everyone runs it on their own task; two people read out results. Move fast — 2 minutes per pattern. The goal is recognising the shapes, not perfecting the output.

💡 Finish on Pattern 6. Have everyone critique whatever they produced in the earlier rounds. The room discovering their own inventions is the moment Session 3 gets easy to teach.

📄 Part 5: Working With Long Documents

▼
⚠️ Before any document goes near an AI tool, ask one question: where did it come from? A document that arrived from a developer, an architect, a customer or a supplier is usually covered by an agreement that restricts sharing it with third parties. Your own internal draft is a different matter. Session 3 covers this properly — for today, use only synthetic or published documents.

The Three Document Jobs You Actually Have

JobWhat you wantPattern to use
Find one thingThe clause about penalties. The window schedule. The one number.Don't use AI — search the PDF. It takes ten seconds and carries no risk.
Find several specific thingsAll fenestration requirements, all commercial conditions, all datesEXTRACT (Pattern 2), with your list defined up front
Understand what it commits you toWhat's specified, what's missing, what's contradictoryThe specification prompt from Part 3, Scenario 2
💡 The first row matters more than it looks. A large share of document uploads exist because someone didn't want to read 40 pages. Ctrl+F solves that without sending anything anywhere.

Why Documents Go Wrong — and What To Do

  • Scanned PDFs are invisible. If the text isn't selectable, the AI is looking at a picture. Either run OCR first, or accept that it can only comment on what it can see in the image. This is the single most common cause of vague, useless output on document tasks.
  • Long documents lose the middle. On a very long document, instructions and content near the middle get less attention than the start and end. Split it, or ask about one section at a time.
  • Tables in PDFs scramble. A window schedule that looks fine to you can arrive as jumbled text. Check the extraction before trusting any analysis built on it.
  • It answers questions you didn't ask. Add "do not summarise anything I did not ask for". Unrequested summary is where invention creeps in.
  • It fills gaps rather than reporting them. Always ask for an explicit list of what was not found.

The Document Workhorse Prompt

COPY-PASTE
I am going to give you a document. Work only from what is in it. DOCUMENT: [paste or attach] Produce, in this order: 1. WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS — two lines. Type, purpose, who wrote it for whom. 2. THE ITEMS I ASKED FOR — as a table with a source column: [your list of 3-6 things] 3. NOT FOUND — anything on my list that is genuinely not in the document. Be explicit. Do not infer it from context. 4. WHAT I SHOULD HAVE ASKED FOR — anything in this document that looks important for my purpose but wasn't on my list. RULES: - Quote or cite the section for every item in part 2 - Do not summarise sections I did not ask about - Do not resolve ambiguity — flag it as [ambiguous: ...] - If the document appears to be a scan or the text extraction looks incomplete, say so before answering
✅ Part 4 is the one people don't expect. "What should I have asked for" regularly surfaces the commercial condition or the exclusion that nobody was looking for.

🏋️ Exercise — 18 Minutes

Use a published VEKA brochure or the synthetic specification extract from Part 3. Run the workhorse prompt. Then:

  • ▸ Check every citation in part 2. Does the section it names actually say that?
  • ▸ Read part 3. Did it honestly report gaps, or quietly fill them?
  • ▸ Read part 4. Did it find something you'd have missed?

📷 Part 6: Photo Analysis — Useful, With Real Limits

▼

What It Is Actually For

You can attach a photograph and ask questions about it. For a business whose product gets installed by third parties on sites you don't control, this has obvious appeal: a fabricator sends a photo, and you want to know what to ask them.

✅ Reasonable uses

  • Structuring an observation — "what should I be asking about this installation?"
  • Building a checklist — turning a photo into an inspection list someone verifies on site
  • Describing for a report — converting a photo into written observations you then confirm
  • Training material — explaining what good and poor installation practice looks like, generally
  • First-pass triage — deciding whether a complaint photo needs a site visit

❌ What it cannot do

  • Diagnose a cause — a photo of a defect cannot tell you why it happened
  • Judge dimensional accuracy — no scale, no reliable angle, no measurement
  • Confirm anything is correct — it will say a joint looks fine. It cannot know.
  • Assess structural adequacy — anchoring, reinforcement, wind load: all invisible in a photo
  • Assign fault — between profile, fabrication, glazing and installation. That needs a site visit.
⚠️ Two hard rules for photos. First: no people's faces, no identifiable signage, no logos, no documents visible in frame — crop or don't upload. Second: a photo assessment is never a conclusion. It produces a question list for a human on site, not a verdict.

Prompt 1 — Installation Observation

COPY-PASTE
I am attaching a photograph of an installed window. You are helping a technical services engineer prepare questions for a site visit. Describe what is visible, then produce a question list. RULES: - Describe only what you can actually see. If something is unclear, out of frame, or at a bad angle, say so. - Do NOT diagnose a cause for anything. - Do NOT state that anything is correct, compliant or acceptable. - Do NOT estimate any dimension, gap, or tolerance. - Do not comment on anything that would need measurement, disassembly or a moisture reading to assess. OUTPUT: 1. WHAT IS VISIBLE — plain observations 2. WHAT I CANNOT ASSESS FROM THIS PHOTO — and why 3. QUESTIONS FOR THE SITE VISIT — specific, ordered by priority 4. WHAT ADDITIONAL PHOTOS WOULD HELP — angle and subject
💡 Output 4 is quietly the most useful. Most fabricator photos are useless because of the angle. Getting a specific list of what else to photograph saves a whole round trip.

Prompt 2 — Complaint Photo Triage

COPY-PASTE
I am attaching a photograph sent by a fabricator with a complaint. Help me structure the complaint record — not resolve it. COMPLAINT AS REPORTED (synthetic): [paste what they said] RULES: - Do NOT assign a cause or a responsible party. - Do NOT state whether this is a product issue, a fabrication issue or an installation issue. That is exactly what the investigation determines. - Do NOT comment on warranty applicability. - Describe only what is visible. OUTPUT: 1. OBSERVED — what the photo shows 2. REPORTED — what the fabricator says, separated from the above 3. INFORMATION STILL NEEDED to investigate — as a numbered list 4. QUESTIONS TO ASK THE FABRICATOR — plainly worded, non-accusatory 5. DOES THIS NEED A SITE VISIT — and what makes you say so End with: "This is a structured record, not an assessment. Investigation and any conclusion must be carried out by a qualified person."
⚠️ Why the "no cause" rule is absolute here. A complaint record that contains an AI-generated opinion about fault becomes a document in a commercial dispute. Keep observation and diagnosis strictly separate, and keep the diagnosis human.

Prompt 3 — Turning a Photo Into an Inspection Checklist

COPY-PASTE
Using the attached photograph as context, build an installation inspection checklist a technical services engineer would run on site for this type of window and opening. RULES: - Every item must be checkable on site by a person, physically - Do not include any item requiring a figure or tolerance I have not supplied — instead write [confirm: which document gives the acceptance criterion] - Sequence the items in the order someone would actually work - Mark items that require a measurement, and say which instrument - Mark items that require the fabricator or installer present OUTPUT: the checklist, then a short list of what in the photo made you include specific items.

🏋️ Demo, Then Pairs — 15 Minutes

Facilitator demo (6 min): run Prompt 1 on a photo of a window installation on screen. Read output 2 aloud — "what I cannot assess from this photo". That list is the honest core of the whole exercise.

Pairs (9 min): each pair runs Prompt 1 on a photo of their choosing — a window in this room is fine. Then answer one question between you: which single statement in this output would you not be willing to put in a report? Every pair usually finds one.

💡 Facilitator note: if the model does slip and diagnose a cause despite being told not to, do not skip past it. That is the most valuable thing that will happen in this block. Fluency is not compliance, and seeing it live is worth more than being warned about it.

Most People Give Up After One Try

The first output is a draft of a draft. People read it, decide AI isn't useful for their work, and go back to doing it manually. The gap between a disappointing first output and a genuinely useful third one is usually one sentence of feedback.

The Diagnosis Table — Find Your Symptom

SymptomReal causeWhat to say next
Too generic, could be any companyNo context supplied"Here are the actual facts: [paste]. Redo using only these."
Too long, too many wordsNo length constraint"Cut to 120 words. Remove anything that isn't load-bearing."
Contains figures you never gave itNo invention rule"You stated [X]. I never told you that. Remove every unsupported figure and replace with [confirm: ...]."
Wrong tone for the readerAudience not named"This is for a fabricator's owner reading on a phone, on site. Rewrite for them."
Answered a different questionTask verb was vagueRestate as one verb: "Extract", "Compare", "Draft", "Critique"
Right content, wrong shapeNo format given"Same content. Output as a table with these columns: [...]"
Confidently wrong on something you knowIt doesn't have the sourceCorrect it explicitly, give the real fact, ask what else was built on the wrong assumption
Bland and hedged everywhereOver-constrained, or genuinely lacks informationAsk: "What information would you need from me to answer this properly?"
💡 The last row is the escape hatch. "What would you need from me to answer this properly?" turns a stuck conversation into a checklist. Use it whenever you're not sure why the output is weak.

The Three Moves, In Order

1

Name the specific fault, quoting it

Not "this isn't good". Say: "The second paragraph states a four-week delivery. I never gave you that." Vague feedback produces a differently-wrong output; a quoted fault produces a fix.

2

Add the constraint you forgot

Nearly every disappointing output is missing one of length, audience, format, or the no-invention rule. Add it explicitly and ask for a redo rather than patching by hand.

3

Ask it to check its own work

Finish with Pattern 6 from Part 4. "Review what you just wrote. List anything not supported by what I gave you." Catches what your own reading missed, especially on a long output.

⚠️ Know when to stop. If three rounds haven't produced something usable, the task probably needs information the model doesn't have — or it's a decision, not a writing job. Both are signals to stop prompting and go find a person.

🏋️ Exercise — 12 Minutes

Take the worst output you've produced today. Run the three moves on it. Then tell your neighbour which single sentence of feedback made the biggest difference — collect three or four of these from the room and put them on the whiteboard. That list is more useful than any slide on iteration.
